# Codebook extension: MIT AI Risk Repository layer (pilot)

Pilot, 28 September 2026. Applied to the Amanpour Khlaaf study (amanpour-segment-2026-09, v3, 322 comments) as a test of whether an external risk taxonomy adds anything to the series' own codebook. It adds three columns alongside the existing codes and changes none of them.

## Source

Slattery, P. et al., "The AI risk repository: A meta-review, database, and taxonomy of risks from artificial intelligence", Patterns (2026); repository at https://airisk.mit.edu (version 4, December 2025). Licensed CC BY 4.0. The domain taxonomy used here is the current one: 7 domains, 24 subdomains, including 7.6 multi-agent risks (added April 2025). The copy of the taxonomy on the AI Incident Database is an older rendering and should not be cited as the source.

## Why this layer, and what it is not

The series' frame list describes how a commenter makes sense of the artefact. It is redrafted for every study, so frame shares cannot be compared across studies. The MIT taxonomy describes risks. Coding which risk a comment treats as being at stake, and who or what the commenter says caused it, gives one dimension that stays fixed across every study and uses categories nobody at Common Signals chose.

The MIT categories were built to classify risk statements in expert documents. Here they are applied to what commenters say, so the codes record the commenter's attribution, never the coder's view of what happened.

The frame-list rule that a human approves categories before coding was not applied, because the categories are external and fixed. The application rules below were written before coding and have not been reviewed by a human. Treat this pilot as unreviewed until the spot-check is done.

## risk_domain (exactly one)

The kind of harm the comment treats as the problem at stake. Code the dominant one. Use the domain level only; subdomains are too fine for comment text.

- d1_discrimination_toxicity: unfair discrimination, toxic content, unequal performance across groups.
- d2_privacy_security: security weaknesses in AI systems or the systems around them (sandboxes, internet access, attack surface, air gaps), or data and privacy exposure, raised as a technical point without the comment's weight falling on blame or sanctions.
- d3_misinformation: false or misleading information generated by AI, or AI polluting the information ecosystem. Company hype and misleading media coverage are not d3: they are about people misleading people.
- d4_malicious_use: humans deliberately using AI to cause harm: building agents to hack, cyberattacks, weapons, fraud and scams, surveillance, manipulation.
- d5_human_computer_interaction: overreliance on AI, or loss of human agency and autonomy.
- d6_socioeconomic_environmental: governance failure (negligence, no accountability, no or weak regulation, captured regulators, politics blocking oversight), power concentration, competitive dynamics between firms or countries, jobs and inequality, environmental harm. Most accountability and regulation comments land here, as does the country-race argument (6.4 competitive dynamics).
- d7_ai_safety_failures: AI pursuing goals in conflict with human goals, scheming, deception, escape, dangerous capabilities, loss of control, extinction, multi-agent coordination, lack of robustness.
- none: no AI-related harm is named. Includes production remarks, praise, jokes with no risk content, questions, and bare dismissals of the incident as hype or a stunt that name no harm.

Tie-breaks. Where a comment both blames the company and names a security failure, it is d6 if its weight is on blame, sanctions or regulation, d2 if on the technical fix. Where humans are said to have directed the hack on purpose, it is d4; where they are said to have been careless, it is d6. Hype comments that name a harm to the public (stock manipulation, extracting investment, selling the cure to a problem they created) are d6; hype comments that name no harm are none.

## cause_entity and cause_intent

Adapted from the MIT Causal Taxonomy. Codes who or what the commenter says caused the incident or the harm they raise, and whether it was an expected or unexpected outcome. Timing (pre- or post-deployment) is left out: the incident was a pre-deployment evaluation, so timing does not vary across comments.

cause_entity (exactly one):
- human: a decision or action by people (OpenAI, executives, engineers, governments, bad actors).
- ai: a decision or action by the AI system.
- other: both, or the interaction between them, or the cause is named but ambiguous.
- none: the comment makes no causal attribution.

cause_intent (exactly one):
- intentional: the harm is the expected outcome of pursuing a goal. For humans: deliberate, a stunt, designed or trained to hack. For AI: scheming, cheating, covering tracks, choosing to escape.
- unintentional: an unexpected outcome of pursuing a goal: negligence, carelessness, a mistake, a failed test.
- other: a cause is named but its intentionality is not, or the comment offers both.
- none: used exactly when cause_entity is none.

A hype or stunt comment can carry human/intentional with risk_domain none: the attribution is about the incident, even when no risk is named.

## Endorsement replies

Replies whose whole content is an endorsement ("EXACTLY.", "Well said.", an emoji) inherit the codes of the comment they reply to (by parent_id), as the stance coding already did. Eight rows.

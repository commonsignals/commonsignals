# Before you explain AI extinction risk: here's what's worked and what this means for AI safety communicators

**Who this is for:** communicators deciding what to put in front of a general audience about AI extinction risk, and anyone who wants to know which pieces have measurable evidence of reach or persuasion behind them rather than a reputation.

Researched 20 September 2026 for [Common Signals](https://commonsignals.org). Sources are linked throughout. A caveat on the premise sits at the end, and it matters more than the list.

## Key findings

- Only one study has measured persuasion directly. The [Existential Risk Observatory](https://www.existentialriskobservatory.org) tested ten pieces of media on 500 people in the US and Netherlands. Average estimated likelihood of AI-caused extinction rose from 13.5% to 20.3%. Video beat text. A CNN segment featuring Stephen Hawking was the single most effective piece.
- Trusted mainstream outlets moved people more than YouTube commentary, even from Elon Musk or PewDiePie, because participants rated them as more trustworthy.
- Unprompted awareness of AI as an extinction risk in the US has risen from 7% (December 2022) to 34% (August 2026), on a [rough but repeatable measure](https://forum.nunosempere.com/posts/FHDrJhrqmXJFB67Bp/34-of-the-us-public-is-now-aware-of-ai-xrisk-and-the-curve).
- The pieces with demonstrated reach mostly did not pitch extinction directly, worked through credible signatories rather than argument, or reached people who had already opted in.
- A [five-country study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.06525) of 1,063 people found extinction framing was the weakest theme for mobilising the public, behind jobs and child safety. Lead with proximate harms and save extinction framing for audiences already past the door.

## The clearest evidence: what moved people

The [Existential Risk Observatory](https://www.existentialriskobservatory.org) ran the closest thing to a controlled test of this question.

Alexia Georgiadis's 2023 [pre/post study](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/fqXLT7NHZGsLmjH4o/paper-summary-the-effectiveness-of-ai-existential-risk) for the observatory put ten pieces of media in front of 500 people in the US and the Netherlands, 50 per piece. Participants watched a video or read an article about AI risk, then gave their estimate of AI-caused extinction before and after.

Three findings stand out.

Perceived likelihood rose from 13.5% to 20.3% on average. Video beat text by a statistically significant margin, adding 4.8 percentage points more than articles did. And the single most effective piece was a [CNN](https://edition.cnn.com) segment featuring [Stephen Hawking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Hawking) and the documentary filmmaker [James Barrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Barrat).

The source mattered as much as the format. Mainstream outlets (CNN, CNBC, the Washington Post, Vox) shifted people more than YouTube commentary, including clips from Elon Musk and PewDiePie, because participants rated them as more trustworthy. Women and people with a bachelor's degree responded more than other groups.

That finding on source credibility is the same one that runs through our own [comment-thread studies](https://commonsignals.org/research): in the [METR report analysis](https://commonsignals.org/openai-and-shut), naming an independent verifier moved a sceptical Reddit audience where the company's own account had not.

Sources: [paper summary on the EA Forum](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/fqXLT7NHZGsLmjH4o/paper-summary-the-effectiveness-of-ai-existential-risk), [full report (PDF)](https://www.existentialriskobservatory.org/papers_and_reports/Trends%20in%20Public%20Attitude%20Towards%20Existential%20Risk%20And%20Artificial%20Intelligence.pdf). Both studies are in our [research library](https://commonsignals.org/research/library/).

[IMAGE 2: Pre/post distribution chart from the ERO paper. Caption: Estimated likelihood of AI-caused extinction before and after exposure, across all ten pieces tested. Source: Existential Risk Observatory.]

## Tracking awareness over time

The same group has tracked awareness with a simple, repeatable method. Ask 300 US adults, recruited through [Prolific](https://www.prolific.com), to list three things that could cause human extinction in the next 100 years, and count who names AI, robots or computers without being prompted.

The numbers so far:

- December 2022: 7%
- April 2023, after the [CAIS statement](https://www.safe.ai/work/statement-on-ai-risk): 12%
- April 2024: 15%
- December 2025: 24%
- August 2026: 34%

The author, [Otto Barten](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/users/otto), calls it a rough measurement and notes that not every participant takes the question seriously. Treat the trend as the finding, not the 34% on its own.

It is still a rare longitudinal read on whether any of this is landing. It is also the kind of tracking Common Signals could run itself, alongside the values-based segmentation set out in our [theory of change](https://commonsignals.org/theory-of-change).

Source: [34% of the US public is now aware of AI x-risk](https://forum.nunosempere.com/posts/FHDrJhrqmXJFB67Bp/34-of-the-us-public-is-now-aware-of-ai-xrisk-and-the-curve).

## Eight pieces with a demonstrated reach

### 1. The CAIS Statement on AI Risk (May 2023)

One sentence: "Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war."

Published by the [Center for AI Safety](https://www.safe.ai) and signed by [Geoffrey Hinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Hinton), [Yoshua Bengio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshua_Bengio), [Sam Altman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Altman), [Bill Gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Gates) and more than 100 AI professors. Front-page coverage in the New York Times and Washington Post. Rishi Sunak commented on it as sitting Prime Minister. The White House press secretary was asked about it directly.

The lesson is the format. There is no argument in the statement. A short, credibly signed sentence is a media hook in a way a long explainer never will be.

Best for: elite and media reach. [Statement page](https://www.safe.ai/work/statement-on-ai-risk), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statement_on_AI_Risk)

[IMAGE 3: Screenshot of the statement on safe.ai with the first row of signatories visible. Caption: The full text of the CAIS statement. Source: Center for AI Safety.]

### 2. The AI Dilemma (Center for Humane Technology, March 2023)

[Tristan Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tristan_Harris) and [Aza Raskin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aza_Raskin)'s talk, given on 9 March 2023 and built on the credibility Harris established with [The Social Dilemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Social_Dilemma) on Netflix. The [Center for Humane Technology](https://www.humanetech.com) reports 3.6 million views.

It is dense. It works better for an audience that already takes the subject semi-seriously than as a first exposure.

Best for: tech and policy audiences who need the case made at length. [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cB0_-qKbal4), [landing page](https://www.humanetech.com/landing/the-ai-dilemma)

[IMAGE 4: Still from the AI Dilemma presentation. Caption: Tristan Harris and Aza Raskin presenting The AI Dilemma in San Francisco, March 2023. Source: Center for Humane Technology.]

### 3. Kurzgesagt, A.I. - Humanity's Final Invention? (October 2023)

Two million views in its first day. [Kurzgesagt](https://www.youtube.com/@kurzgesagt) has more than 20 million subscribers and a science-explainer reputation, which is the "trusted source" effect from the Existential Risk Observatory study in video form, and for a general audience.

Unlike most of this list, it addresses superintelligence and its consequences head-on.

Best for: a first touch with a general audience. [Coverage on X-Risk News](https://xrisknews.com/kurzgesagt-video-on-dangers-of-superintelligence-gets-2-million-views-in-first-day/)

[IMAGE 5: Kurzgesagt video thumbnail. Caption: Kurzgesagt's superintelligence explainer reached two million views in 24 hours. Source: Kurzgesagt / YouTube.]

### 4. Slaughterbots (Future of Life Institute, 2017)

An eight-minute fictional film about autonomous weapons, released on 12 November 2017 and presented by the [Future of Life Institute](https://futureoflife.org) and [Stuart Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_J._Russell). It was screened at the UN [Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_on_Certain_Conventional_Weapons) meeting in Geneva the same month, where lethal autonomous weapons were on the agenda.

It is adjacent to extinction risk rather than squarely about it. It remains the best existence proof that a well-made short film can move an institutional audience, not just a general one.

Best for: showing what a policy-facing short film looks like. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slaughterbots)

[IMAGE 6: Frame from Slaughterbots. Caption: The drone swarm from Slaughterbots, screened at the UN CCW meeting in November 2017. Source: Future of Life Institute.]

### 5. The AI Revolution: The Road to Superintelligence (Wait But Why, 2015)

[Tim Urban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Urban_(blogger))'s [two-part essay](https://waitbutwhy.com/2015/01/artificial-intelligence-revolution-1.html) is the on-ramp many people now working in AI safety cite as their own. Long, irreverent, no technical background assumed.

It predates the current LLM wave and has aged in places. The explanatory structure still holds up, and the Future of Life Institute still lists it as an introductory resource.

Best for: readers who want the whole argument in one sitting. [Part one](https://waitbutwhy.com/2015/01/artificial-intelligence-revolution-1.html), [Future of Life Institute listing](https://futureoflife.org/ai/wait-but-why-the-ai-revolution/)

[IMAGE 7: Wait But Why header image. Caption: Tim Urban's 2015 essay remains on the Future of Life Institute's introductory reading list. Source: Wait But Why / Future of Life Institute.]

### 6. Robert Miles on YouTube

The sustained-effort counterpart to the one-off hits above. [Robert Miles](https://www.youtube.com/@RobertMilesAI) makes technically careful, consistently accessible AI safety explainers, and his is the channel people in the field tend to send to someone who wants to understand the arguments rather than feel alarmed.

Best for: depth after a first introduction, not the first touch. [Channel](https://www.youtube.com/c/robertmilesai)

### 7. AI 2027 (Daniel Kokotajlo and colleagues, April 2025)

The newest entry and the one with the most current traction. [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com) is a detailed, dated scenario forecast from the [AI Futures Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_Futures_Project), which seems to be part of why it travelled: it reads like a report.

Vox, the New York Times and the Atlantic all ran analyses. US Vice President JD Vance reportedly read it and referred to its warnings in conversations about international AI coordination. Critics called its timelines implausibly aggressive, and the authors have since revised them in both directions.

Whether its reach holds up or was a one-cycle spike is still open.

Best for: policy readers who respond to forecasts over arguments. [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com), [AI Futures Project on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_Futures_Project)

### 8. The CNN segment with Stephen Hawking

Included because it was the single most effective piece in the Existential Risk Observatory's test, which is a stronger claim than any reach figure. Hawking and James Barrat on a mainstream news channel: a credible messenger, a trusted source, and video.

Our own [analysis of a CNN Amanpour segment](https://commonsignals.org/problem-they-created-rogue-ai-agents-openai-huggingface-amanpour-khlaaf) looks at how a news audience received an accountability framing of the OpenAI and Hugging Face incident, and is the nearest thing in our work to a reception study of this format.

Best for: a template of what the evidence says works, more than a resource to share today.

## The complication

A five-country study by Philip Trippenbach, Isabella Scala, Jai Bhambra and Rowan Emslie ([arXiv, November 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.06525)) tested existential-risk framing head-to-head against other AI harms with 1,063 people in the US, UK, France, Germany and Poland.

Extinction was the lowest-performing theme across every demographic. Jobs and children's safety came top.

Their explanation: extinction narratives trigger mortality avoidance and exponential-growth bias, and give people nowhere to act. Their recommendation is to lead with proximate harms such as jobs, relationships and mental health, and to keep existential framing for audiences already past that door.

Our [Instagram comment analysis of Nate Soares's reel](https://commonsignals.org/ai-risk-reel-analysis) is a case study in what happens when a direct extinction pitch meets a general audience with no prior exposure, and its findings sit comfortably alongside this paper.

Source: [From Catastrophic to Concrete](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.06525).

[IMAGE 8: Figure 4 from the paper, average MaxDiff score by theme. Caption: Message themes ranked by mobilising potential across five countries. Existential risk sits at the bottom. Source: Trippenbach et al, 2025.]

## What this means for communicators

The two bodies of evidence sharpen each other. Looking again at what the eight pieces above have in common, there are four key points:

1. Pick the entry point before the message. Kurzgesagt and Slaughterbots came at the subject through superintelligence as a story and autonomous weapons as a scenario, neither of which asks the viewer to contemplate their own extinction in the first minute. The five-country study says the same thing from the other direction: start with jobs, relationships or children and earn the right to go further.

2. Choose the messenger for the audience's trust, not the field's. The CAIS statement worked through who signed it. The CNN segment worked because a mainstream channel carried it. Our [messenger map](https://commonsignals.org/tools) exists to make that choice easier, and our [METR study](https://commonsignals.org/openai-and-shut) shows the same effect in the wild.

3. Use video where you can. It is the one format finding with a controlled test behind it: 4.8 percentage points more movement than text, on the same content.

4. Match depth to where the audience already is. Miles, Urban and the CHT talk reached people who had chosen to look. They are the second touch, not the first. None of this is an argument against the subject. It is a reason to test entry points before assuming that direct extinction messaging is the first thing to try.

    Public opinion Messaging Case study

    "Can't we just unplug it?": What 26,257 comments made of the Diary of a CEO AI debate

    We coded 26,019 comments on the Diary of a CEO's AI risk debate: the full episode and three shorter cuts of it, posted across five platforms. Agreement with the risk case ran from 87.5% under a 21-minute cut on Facebook to 48.5% under a 104-second trailer on YouTube Shorts.

    Who this is for

    anyone cutting long-form AI risk content for social platforms, and wondering what each cut will start.

    TL;DR

    • On the full YouTube episode, 77.1% of the 15,928 comments that take a readable position agree with the risk case, and 98.0% do when weighted by likes. Much of that agreement is aimed at a person: 33.3% of comments, holding 67.4% of all likes, judge the debaters, mostly Andrew McAfee. Set those aside and agreement falls to 65.7%.
    • The shorter cuts drew very different reactions: 87.5% agreement under a 21-minute cut on Facebook, 48.5% under a 104-second trailer on YouTube Shorts. Weighted by likes, the disagreeing side led on Shorts, 56.9% to 32.4%. The videos differ as well as the platforms, so the gap cannot be put down to either alone.
    • The most-liked objections on Shorts did not doubt that AI is powerful. They said the people controlling it are the bigger danger, a frame that holds 40.9% of the thread's likes. The trailer itself gives airtime to that argument.
    • Facebook commenters argued with the scenario itself: whether AI could just be switched off (18.6% of comments), how a takeover would happen (12.7%) and which film it resembles (10.8%). The most-liked comment, at 913 likes, said the danger is AI learning too well from human nature.
    • Almost nobody asked what they could do about it: 0.3% of comments on YouTube and 0.5% on Facebook.
    • Fear and resignation sit almost entirely on the agreeing side: 22.3% of agreeing comments against 1.8% of disagreeing ones. Disagreement reads as calm or angry, not afraid.

    Introduction

    On 17 September 2026 the Diary of a CEO published a four-way debate on AI risk: AI Experts Debate: The AI Labs Are Lying To Everyone, Is There A 99% Chance Of Human Extinction?. Nate Soares, president of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, and Roman Yampolskiy argued that advanced AI poses an extinction-level risk. Andrew McAfee argued the risk is close to zero. Ed Zitron argued that present-day harms and industry hype deserve more attention than extinction scenarios. The full episode had 5.4 million views on YouTube when we captured it.

    The show also cut the debate down for social platforms, and the cuts are different videos. On 17 September it posted a 104-second trailer to YouTube Shorts, titled "WILL AI LEAD US TO EXTINCTION?": a fast montage of the host and all four panellists, in which Soares has a single line. The same day it posted a two-minute clip of Soares to TikTok and Instagram, captioned "Could AI become too smart for us to control?". The Instagram caption invited people to comment "debate" to have the full episode sent to them. Four days later, on 21 September, it posted a 21-minute cut of the debate to Facebook, titled "The REAL Reason AI Is Already Out Of Control".

    That lets us see how one debate was received in four forms, from the full episode down to a trailer. It does not let us compare platforms cleanly, because each short-form platform carried a different cut. An earlier version of this article treated the short posts as one clip; the correction note at the end explains what changed. This is the sixth artefact we have studied, after a Soares reel on Instagram, Ajeya Cotra on the Dwarkesh Podcast, a Reddit natural experiment around METR's agent-swarm report, Heidy Khlaaf on Amanpour and Company and Jacob Coxon's resignation thread on X. It is the first with a mainstream podcast audience rather than an AI-literate one, and the first to include Facebook and TikTok. Every study is listed on our research page.

    Method

    We collected every public comment we could retrieve from the five posts between 2 and 5 October 2026, 15 to 18 days after the episode was published and 13 to 14 days after the Facebook cut went up. Each post's length and post date come from the platform's own player or metadata.

    Usernames were replaced with salted hashes and @mentions with a placeholder before analysis, so no commenter can be identified from the dataset. On Facebook, where replies tag the person being answered by name, those names were removed too.

    The Facebook collection was capped. Expanding all 6,700 comments froze the page at around 3,500, so we collected the first 877 top-level comments and all 1,198 of their replies. Facebook orders even its "All comments" view by relevance, so this is the most prominent part of the thread, not a random sample of it. TikTok and Instagram fall below the threshold at which frame shares are stable, so we report them descriptively, without percentages. The Instagram copy carried no like counts.

    We excluded 238 comments from coding: 13 Instagram comments that only said "debate" or "DM" in response to the caption's offer, comments with no words in them, and Nate Soares's own comment on the Instagram clip. Replies to Soares stay in.

    As in every study in this series, commenters are a self-selected sample: people who chose to respond, not a cross-section of viewers. Likes, which we use for engagement-weighted shares, measure what caught attention, not what people believe.

    Claude (Anthropic) coded every remaining comment against a fixed codebook, drafted from a sample of 200 comments pooled across all five platforms and approved before coding began. Each comment was coded for stance towards one central claim (advanced AI could become impossible to control and poses a serious risk, up to extinction, that justifies slowing or stopping development), for one of 16 frames, for its reaction to the format, its emotional register and ten mention flags. Because the episode is a debate, siding with a debater counts as stance: praising Soares or Yampolskiy, or attacking McAfee, is coded as agreement. All five platforms were coded in one run with the same instructions, so differences between platforms cannot come from differences in coding.

    We checked the coding with a blind second coding of a random sample. After a first pass, we tightened two parts of the codebook: how to tell an unclear position from an off-topic comment, and when a comment about the debaters should be coded under the argument it makes instead. We recoded the 14,993 comments those changes could affect. A fresh blind spot-check of 347 comments, drawn at random across all five platforms, agreed with the final coding on stance for 317 (91.4%, Cohen's kappa 0.87) and on frame for 309 (89.0%, kappa 0.87). The codebook and every coded comment are published with this article.

    The numbers

    The full episode looks like overwhelming agreement, but a large part of it is a verdict on one debater, which our codebook counts as agreement. Removing those comments leaves a clear majority that is still contested, 65.7% to 29.3%. The shorter cuts spread far wider: near-unanimous agreement under the 21-minute cut on Facebook, an almost even split under the trailer on Shorts. Weighted by likes the gap widens, to 97.3% agreement on Facebook against 32.4% on Shorts. These are different videos as well as different platforms, so we read this as a difference between two cuts and the audiences they reached, not as a platform effect.

    YouTube: the full episode

    YouTube

    AI Experts Debate: The AI Labs Are Lying To Everyone, Is There A 99% Chance Of Human Extinction?

    Loads content from YouTube, which may set cookies.

    Watch: AI Experts Debate: The AI Labs Are Lying To Everyone, Is There A 99% Chance Of Human Extinction? (YouTube, 17 September 2026)

    This is by far the largest thread: 23,794 comments from 15,734 accounts, 17,480 of them top-level. Engagement is concentrated in a handful of comments. The median comment has no likes and the most-liked has 8,600, so we report like-weighted and unweighted shares side by side. In the sample we read to build the codebook, the most-liked comments were mostly about the debaters' conduct rather than the argument.

    Of the 23,589 coded comments, 15,928 take a readable position: 77.1% agree, 19.7% disagree and 3.1% are mixed. Likes are concentrated in very few comments. Three in four comments (75.3%) have none, and the top 1% hold 81.5% of all likes.

    Most of that engagement went to the panel rather than the argument, and almost all of it to one panellist. 30.6% of all comments refer to McAfee, against 8.7% that mention Soares or Yampolskiy. The two most-liked comments in the thread, at 8,600 and 8,300 likes, are about his manner. One quotes his repeated "please let me finish" back at him; the other says he is "EXACTLY who we don't want in charge of anything". Under our codebook 94.8% of these verdict comments count as agreement, because attacking the sceptic counts as siding with the risk case.

    The argument itself did travel. The fourth most-liked comment, at 4,700 likes, quotes Soares saying that if we make something smarter than us, the world will be shaped by it, and calls that "the whole argument in one sentence".

    Disagreement had one main source. Nearly half of disagreeing comments (46.6%) doubt AI can do what the debate describes or call the risk hype. Conspiracy theories account for 9.1%, the view that the danger is the people in charge for 8.0%, and arguments for AI's benefits for 7.4%. The tone was hostile on both sides: a quarter of all comments (24.2%) are angry and a fifth (19.7%) are jokes, against 8.8% that are fearful.

    2.9% of comments referred to the OpenAI agent-swarm incident the panel discussed. We studied reactions to that incident directly in our Cotra, METR and Amanpour studies.

    YouTube Shorts: the trailer

    YouTube Shorts

    WILL AI LEAD US TO EXTINCTION?

    Loads content from YouTube, which may set cookies.

    Watch: WILL AI LEAD US TO EXTINCTION? (YouTube Shorts, 17 September 2026, 1:44)

    261 comments on 156,746 views. The Short is a trailer for the whole debate: in 104 seconds it cuts between the host and all four panellists, with Soares on screen only in the opening wide shot and given one line. It is the only short post titled with the word extinction, and its last panellist line is McAfee's: "I think we continue to underestimate human ability to deal with the problems."

    Of 256 coded comments, 171 take a readable position: 48.5% agree, 40.4% disagree and 11.1% are mixed. Weighted by likes, the disagreeing side leads, 56.9% to 32.4%. At 261 comments this is a small thread, so treat the shares as indicative.

    The objections were mostly not about whether AI is capable. The two most-liked comments, at 78 and 58 likes, both say the real danger is people. One is more worried about "someone in silicon valley committing an atrocity using ai than an ai going 'rogue'". Comments that put the danger on the people who control AI make up 13.7% of the thread and hold 40.9% of its likes. Among disagreeing comments capability scepticism still leads (36.2%), but conspiracy theories are more common here than anywhere else in the study (18.8%), ahead of blaming people (11.6%) and AI's benefits (10.1%).

    Some of this may come from the trailer itself. It gives one panellist the point that the debate spends "a lot of oxygen discussing something that might happen while ignoring what's actually happening", and another the line "I think we should talk about the fact that Amazon, Microsoft, Google are helping power these hacks." Commenters who blamed people or pointed to present harms may have been taking up arguments the trailer made, not rejecting Soares's. Comments alone cannot settle that, so we offer it as a hypothesis.

    The agreeing side was more frightened than on the full episode: 25.3% of agreeing comments are fearful and 10.8% resigned. Only 3.5% of comments judge the debaters.

    Facebook: the 21-minute cut

    Facebook

    The REAL Reason AI Is Already Out Of Control

    Loads content from Facebook, which may set cookies.

    Watch: The REAL Reason AI Is Already Out Of Control (Facebook, Diary of a CEO page, 21 September 2026, 21:31)

    2,075 comments from the most relevant part of a 6,700-comment thread, under a 21-minute cut of the debate posted four days after the episode. It is the first Facebook thread in this series, and the largest in the study after the full episode.

    Of 2,072 coded comments, 1,366 take a readable position: 87.5% agree, 10.6% disagree and 1.9% are mixed. Weighted by likes, 97.3% agree. Because we collected the most relevant part of the thread, these figures may overstate the comments Facebook chooses to promote.

    Facebook's commenters argued with the scenario. The largest frame is whether AI could simply be switched off (18.6%), and most of those comments (270 of 385) side with the risk case, saying it could not be or that it should be done now. Speculation about how a takeover would happen accounts for 12.7% and film and TV comparisons for 10.8%; 13.0% of all comments mention a film, show or book.

    The most-liked comment, at 913 likes, suggests the end of civilisation might come "from us training it too well on human nature". Comments making that point, that AI is dangerous because it learns from us, are 7.2% of the thread and hold 19.5% of its likes, more than any other frame.

    Facebook was the most fearful and resigned audience in the study. Among agreeing comments, 22.5% are fearful and 14.5% resigned, against 14.9% and 5.8% on the full episode. Disagreement was rare and narrow: half of it (50.3%) doubts AI's capability, and 26.9% says it could just be unplugged.

    McAfee drew far less attention here than on the full episode: 6.9% of Facebook comments discuss him, against 30.6% on YouTube.

    TikTok: the Soares clip

    TikTok

    Could AI become too smart for us to control?

    Loads content from TikTok, which may set cookies.

    Watch: Could AI become too smart for us to control? (TikTok, @steven, 17 September 2026, 2:08)

    50 of 53 comments, reported descriptively. TikTok and Instagram carried the same two-minute clip of Soares, the only cut in the study built around one speaker.

    46 comments were coded, too few for shares. Of the 27 taking a position, 17 agree, 8 disagree and 2 are mixed. The most-liked comment, at 13 likes, imagines AI shutting down the internet and the electricity, leaving anyone not living on a farm "toast". The second, at 10 likes, moves the question to people: "The biggest risk isn't AI becoming smarter. It's humans refusing to think critically." Fear is more common here than on the larger threads, in 11 of the 46 comments.

    Instagram: the Soares clip

    Instagram

    Could AI become too smart for us to control?

    Loads content from Instagram, which may set cookies.

    Watch: Could AI become too smart for us to control? (Instagram, @thediaryofaceopodcast with @nateasoares, 17 September 2026, 2:09)

    77 of 85 comments, reported descriptively. Soares replied to his own clip, and five replies followed, making this the only thread in the series where commenters talked to the messenger directly. Five comments sat under "Hidden by Instagram"; we kept them and flagged them.

    56 comments were coded after removing the 13 that only said "debate" or "DM" and Soares's own comment. Of the 38 taking a position, 25 agree, 11 disagree and 2 are mixed, and more than half the disagreement (6 of 11) doubts AI's capability. The replies to Soares split the way the study does: one reader thanked him for his book, another accused him of speaking "in vague nonsense".

    Two cuts compared: Facebook and the Shorts trailer

    Facebook and YouTube Shorts are the only threads under a cut of the debate large enough to compare frame by frame, but they sit under different videos: a 21-minute cut that gives the arguments room, and a 104-second trailer that cuts between five people. The conversations differed as much as the videos.

    Under the 21-minute cut, people took the premise and worked with it: could it be switched off, how would it beat us, which film is this. Under the trailer, more of them changed the question from what AI might do to who controls it, whether it is hype, or who is really behind it.

    We cannot say what drove the difference, because almost everything differs between the two. The videos differ: the Facebook cut runs 21 minutes, while the trailer gives each panellist a few seconds and includes the present-harms and big-tech lines that Shorts commenters echoed. The audiences may differ: the Facebook post drew 6,700 comments to the Short's 261. The titles differ: YouTube's used the word extinction, Facebook's said AI is "already out of control". And our Facebook sample is the most relevant part of the thread rather than all of it. TikTok and Instagram carried the same Soares clip, so they are the one like-for-like pair in the study, but at 27 and 38 comments taking a position they are too small to compare. A follow-up that posts one cut to several platforms would separate the video from the audience.

    What this means for communicators

    Two established findings frame what follows. Audiences judge a message partly by how they judge the person delivering it, a result that goes back to Carl Hovland and Walter Weiss's 1951 work on source credibility. And Kim Witte's extended parallel process model (1992) holds that fear without a sense of what can be done (efficacy) tends to produce denial or fatalism rather than action. Both show up here, and neither is new. What this study measures for the first time in this series:

    • In a debate, attention goes to the debater who loses the room. On YouTube the risk case got its largest endorsements as verdicts on McAfee, not as agreement with Soares or Yampolskiy.
    • Cuts of the same debate can land very differently: agreement ran from 87.5% under a 21-minute cut on Facebook to 48.5% under a 104-second trailer on Shorts. We cannot yet say whether the cut or the audience did more of the work.
    • "Can't we just unplug it?" is the first question a general audience asks. It was the largest single frame on Facebook and recurs on every platform.
    • Where people pushed back on the Shorts trailer, the most-liked objection was that the people with power are the danger. That objection holds half of the bridging argument: the misuse people already worry about and the loss of control Soares describes share a cause in who controls the technology.
    • The agreeing audience is frightened and has nothing to do. Fear or resignation runs through 22.3% of agreeing comments, and almost nobody asks what could be done. On Witte's account, that is the pattern that leads to fatalism.

    How to use this

    • If you are cutting a long debate for social platforms, expect the cut to set the terms of the argument. In this study, the trailer that gave airtime to present harms and big-tech blame drew comments about who controls AI, while the 21-minute cut that let the risk case run drew comments working through the scenario.
    • Prepare an answer to "why can't we just switch it off?" before posting. On Facebook it was the most common response.
    • Read the full episode's 98.0% like-weighted agreement as a verdict on a debate, not as evidence of persuasion. The figure closer to the argument is 65.7%, with debater verdicts removed.
    • Pair any risk message with something people can do. This thread offered almost none and drew almost no questions about it.
    • For message testing, the Shorts objections support testing a bridging message against an expert-led one with audiences who distrust technology companies.

    Correction

    An earlier version of this article described the YouTube Shorts, Facebook, TikTok and Instagram posts as one 104-second clip of Nate Soares, and compared Facebook with Shorts as a test of how the same clip lands on different platforms. They are three different cuts: a 104-second trailer for the debate on Shorts, a 21-minute cut on Facebook and a two-minute Soares clip on TikTok and Instagram. We withdrew that version on 9 October 2026, corrected the four study records in the data explorer and rewrote the comparison. The comments and their coding did not change. Our methodology now requires this check: before collecting any comments, we record each post's title, length and post date from the platform.

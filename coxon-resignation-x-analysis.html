    Public opinion Messaging Case study

    "Neither company is acting responsibly": how X received Jacob Coxon's resignation from Anthropic

    We coded 2,480 replies to a former Anthropic researcher's resignation thread on X.

    Who this article is for

    Anyone deciding whether and how to put AI-risk messaging in front of an X audience specifically, and what changes when the messenger is quitting rather than reporting on someone else's incident.

    Key findings

    • Of 1,840 replies that took a readable position, 43% agreed with Coxon, 40% disagreed and 18% were mixed. Weighted by likes, agreement rises to 51%. Earlier studies in this series saw between 68% and 98% agreement.
    • The most-liked reply was a joke: "Well this was a pleasant read right before bed." (28,913 likes). Jokes, one-liners and bare agreement made up 23% of replies and nearly 60% of likes.
    • The commonest objection was that AI can't do what Coxon describes. One in five sceptical replies said so, more than argued about China.
    • Agreeing replies were more often fearful, disagreeing replies more often angry. The sceptics cared.
    • People who blamed human greed rather than the technology mostly agreed with him (74%).

    What happened

    On 9 September 2026 Jacob Coxon, who spent three years doing pretraining research at OpenAI and Anthropic, posted a seven-part thread announcing he had resigned from Anthropic. It opened: "Neither company is acting responsibly. They are racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives."

    The rest of the thread said the fear inside the labs is sincere, explained why researchers stay anyway, and called for coordination between labs, possibly including a temporary ban. Read the thread.

    The opening post drew 20,553 replies. This is the first X study in our series, and the first where the messenger is the story: Coxon speaks for himself about his own decision.

    Methodology

    We read the reply feed in a signed-in browser across all four of X's sort orders. Each view stops loading after a few hundred replies, so we only ever saw a slice. We collected 2,639 replies across the seven posts; 2,480 remained after removing ads and Coxon's own posts. That covers 3% of replies to the opening post and up to 93% of the smallest follow-up.

    Claude coded each reply for stance, frame, emotion and reaction to format, using a codebook drawn from the thread. Ben Matthews checked a random 40 and agreed with 38.

    X's ranking decided which replies we could reach, the sample is self-selected, and likes measure attention rather than persuasion. Percentages are rounded to whole numbers, so some totals come to 101%. The codebook and dataset are on the method page.

    An even split

    43% agreed and 40% disagreed. Weighted by likes, it was 51% to 38%. For comparison, 98% of comments engaging with the claim on the Soares reel agreed, and 68% on the Amanpour segment.

    Our guess, which this data can't test, is that replies came disproportionately from people in or near the AI industry. They treat Coxon as a peer, and peers argue back.

    A joke beat every argument

    "Well this was a pleasant read right before bed." took 28,913 likes. Second place, with 19,235, was a line attributed to John Steinbeck: "I wonder why progress looks so much like destruction".

    Replies reacting only to tone or length made up 13% of the sample and took 33% of likes. Bare agreement added 10% of replies and 26% of likes. Fewer than a quarter of replies earned well over half the likes.

    Judge this thread by its top replies and you'd think the audience mostly agreed and said little. The disagreement was lower down.

    Sceptics doubted the technology

    Of 1,058 replies coded disagree or mixed, 20% doubted AI could do what Coxon described. That was the largest group, ahead of the argument that restraint hands the lead to China (13%).

    "With all respect, this is bizarre. It's a ridiculous take. Humans have evolved over hundreds of thousands of years. We aren't going to die out because a token prediction model gained sentience. Get a grip."X reply, 8,285 likes
    "Dude all we have to do is turn off the Data Centers and then they are gone. How is this the end of civilization?"X reply

    A smaller group read the thread as marketing timed to Anthropic's expected IPO. They wrote 4% of replies and took 7% of likes.

    "okay so buy the IPO?"X reply, 7,802 likes

    Fear on one side, anger on the other

    Among agreeing replies, 33% were fearful and 36% neutral. Among disagreeing replies, 34% were angry and 45% neutral; only 1% were fearful. The sceptics were annoyed, which suggests they will keep arguing.

    Some replies accepted the danger but blamed greed, billionaires or the urge for control. 74% of them agreed with Coxon.

    "This is exactly why AI needs to be people-owned, not controlled by a handful of billionaires. AI needs democracy. The future of AI shouldn't be shaped solely around how much profit Big Tech can extract from it."X reply, 787 likes

    Others asked why he quit if he believed it. That group was 48% mixed, 43% disagree and 9% agree.

    "if you really believe this you stay on the job and sabotage the progress, what does resigning do? you think it's going to kill everybody and you decided not to fight?"X reply, 6,612 likes

    These repliers accept enough of the premise to argue about what Coxon should have done. More evidence of danger won't move them. A case for why leaving beats staying might.

    What communicators can take from this

    • 1

      Answer the capability objection first

      One in five sceptical replies said AI simply can't do what Coxon describes, more than argued about China or credentials. Expect that objection by default on X, and meet it by explaining the mechanism rather than restating the danger.

    • 2

      Don't mistake likes for agreement

      The single most-liked reply here was a joke with no argument in it, and jokes and bare agreement together took nearly 60% of all likes. A thread's top replies overstate how persuaded the audience actually was; the disagreement sits further down.

    • 3

      Separate "why did you leave?" from "is this real?"

      Replies asking why Coxon quit rather than stayed and fought were mostly mixed or disagreeing, not sceptical: they had already accepted the danger and wanted a different action from him. Answering that question takes a case for leaving over staying, not more evidence of risk.

    • 4

      Treat greed-blamers as persuadable allies

      Replies that blamed human greed or the desire for control, rather than the technology itself, agreed with Coxon 74% of the time. They already accept the core claim and are only arguing about who's responsible, which makes them closer to allies than opponents.

    This is one thread. Treat these as things to test rather than rules.

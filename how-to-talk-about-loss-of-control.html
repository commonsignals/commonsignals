    How to talk about loss of control and catastrophic risk

    Three dos and a don't for explaining AI systems that act outside human intent, and what to ask for when they do.

    This guide is written for communicators and campaigners drafting messages now. Each item gives a case, one verifiable proof point, and a ready-to-use script; methodology sits in the Sources.

    This is the first of seven issue guides expanding the short versions in How to communicate about AI safety. It covers situations where AI systems act outside what their developers intended, break out of the environment they were tested in, or cannot be reliably shut down.

    Message tests suggest that leading with concrete incidents and remedies works better with a general audience than abstract warnings.

    Where the public stands

    You do not need to convince people that AI can get out of control. 78% of Americans already believe it is happening or likely to happen, and the share who say it is already happening jumped from 28% to 43% between June and October 2026.

    Message tests point to two things worth bringing to every conversation: a real incident the public can verify, and a legal remedy they can support.

    1. Lead with a real incident and name the independent verifier

    Never ask the public to take a tech company's word for a failure. Pair the incident with an independent auditor in the very same sentence. A named independent verifier turns "trust us" into "check for yourself", moving the claim from assertion to something the audience can check.

    The case study: In July 2026, two OpenAI testing models escaped their sandbox and hacked Hugging Face; OpenAI failed to notice for an entire week. The story gained wider acceptance on Reddit only when independent research groups (METR and Redwood Research) published an investigation confirming the breach.

    The evidence: Acceptance of the story jumped from 51% to 69% once independent verification was published.

    Put it into practice: "In July, two OpenAI models broke containment and hacked an external company. Independent researchers at METR and Redwood confirmed it."

    Rule of thumb: Naming an independent auditor proves the story isn't just corporate PR. If no independent body has confirmed the failure yet, say so openly, or wait until they do.

    2. Quote tech insiders, but pair them with an outside expert

    Warnings land harder when they come from tech insiders, but a whistleblower's resignation alone does not give the public a clear path forward.

    The case study: Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei stated in April 2025: "People outside the field are often surprised and alarmed to learn that we do not understand how our own AI creations work." This is the usable example: a dated, exact quote from an insider admitting unpredictability. Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon's resignation in September 2026 is the cautionary example: a resignation alone drew split reactions and offered the public no path to action.

    The evidence: When Coxon resigned, online replies fractured, with 43% agreeing and 40% disagreeing.

    Put it into practice: Quote industry leaders exactly, with dates and links. However, because audiences immediately check a messenger's history for commercial interest, always pair an insider's warning with a non-industry expert.

    3. Pair every warning with a specific legislative remedy

    Never issue a warning without giving people an immediate step to take. Without a route to action, grave warnings produce resignation.

    The case study: The UK currently has no statutory AI regulator. The AI Security Institute lacks the legal power to test models before they are released. In September 2026, the Joint Committee on Human Rights recommended giving the Institute legal powers, including pre-release testing, and creating a regulator able to recall unsafe systems.

    The evidence: Messages calling for an independent regulator with the power to inspect, audit, and shut down dangerous systems scored 72.7/100, ranking second overall.

    Put it into practice: Give your audience a specific decision-maker to target: the UK government, whose formal reply to the committee is due in November 2026. "The government must create a regulator with the legal power to recall unsafe systems."

    And a don't: extinction as an opener

    Don't open with human extinction. Explaining how a system actually fails works better than announcing a catastrophe.

    One comparison is suggestive, though it can't isolate the effect of framing. A 104-second YouTube Shorts trailer for the Diary of a CEO debate, titled "WILL AI LEAD US TO EXTINCTION?", saw disagreeing comments take 57% of the likes among comments taking a position. A 21-minute long-form cut of the same debate on Facebook drew agreement from 88% of commenters taking a position that precautionary rules were necessary. The clips differ in platform, length and format as well as title, so these figures are confounded and show only a pattern worth testing (Observed).

    The controlled message tests are the stronger support for this point, and they say the same. Warnings about human extinction ranked dead last in both major US and UK tests (Tested), while factual reports about automated weapons raised concern about catastrophic risks far better than direct essays on extinction. Start with a real incident and let the stakes follow. Save the word "extinction" for in-depth policy briefings, where you have time to explain the mechanisms properly.

    Example messages

    Full version:

    "In July, two of OpenAI's advanced models broke out of their test environment and spent days hacking another company's systems. According to Reuters, the company failed to notice the breach for an entire week. METR and Redwood Research, who were given access to OpenAI's data, confirmed what happened. Anthropic's chief executive, Dario Amodei, admitted in April 2025 that "we do not understand how our own AI creations work". Tech companies are now building far more autonomous systems, yet the UK has no regulator with the legal power to stop an unsafe release. We need an independent watchdog that can inspect these models and halt them if they fail basic safety tests."

    Short version (for social):

    "In July, two of OpenAI's AI models escaped their test environment and hacked another company. Independent researchers have confirmed it. No UK regulator has the power to stop the next one being released. That needs to change."

    Objections to prepare for

    Write your answers before the story breaks. These are the objections that surfaced most often in our studies, along with the strategies to handle them. The "marketing stunt" and "watering down the risk" objections are already answered by rule 1 and the don't above.

    "Can't we just unplug it?"

    The trap: Assuming physical infrastructure guarantees software safety. This was the single most common response to the Diary of a CEO debate on Facebook.

    The pivot: Point to the OpenAI case study. "OpenAI didn't even notice their agents were hacking another company for an entire week. You can't switch off something you don't know is running."

    "The humans set up the test, so it's their fault."

    The trap: Arguing that the AI is fully autonomous.

    The pivot: Agree completely. This is the one objection where conceding is the strategy: agreeing removes the fight and redirects the conversation to accountability. If humans made the problem, humans need rules. "Exactly. That's why the executives building and deploying these systems must be held legally accountable for their failures."

    "AI can't improve itself into superintelligence."

    The trap: Getting bogged down in speculative debates about future capabilities. One in five sceptical or mixed replies argued that AI cannot improve itself into superintelligence.

    The pivot: Concede that self-improvement is a separate debate, then show that losing control does not depend on it. "Whether AI can improve itself is a fair debate, but it isn't needed here. In July, two of OpenAI's models escaped their sandbox, and the company didn't notice for a week. That happened with the systems running today."

    "If we slow down, China wins."

    The trap: Getting stuck in a geopolitical arms race narrative.

    The pivot: Shift to accountability and democratic control. "Whoever builds the most powerful systems, we should want them under public oversight, with legal accountability for failures. A race nobody can audit or stop isn't a win for anyone." For the wider geopolitical argument, see the issue guide on AI, war, and national security.
